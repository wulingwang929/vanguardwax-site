Most cars need waxing every one to three months. Garage-kept cars can stretch to three; cars parked outdoors or driven through a rainy season need it monthly. The water test tells you more than the calendar.

## Start with where the car sleeps

There is no single correct interval, because what really burns through a wax layer is the environment the car sits in all day. Use these as starting points:

- **Garage or underground parking, ordinary commuting:** roughly every three months. The paint spends most of its life out of the sun, so four applications a year is usually plenty.
- **Parked outdoors, full sun all day:** roughly every four to eight weeks. Heat radiating off asphalt is brutal on a wax film, and summer is the season to shorten the interval.
- **High mileage, driven daily:** roughly every two months, with extra attention to the front end and the areas behind the mirrors where bugs and tar collect.
- **Coastal areas, parked under trees, heavy bird traffic:** roughly monthly, with quick clean-ups in between washes.
- **Weekend car, stored indoors:** three to four months. Low mileage is not a reason to skip a year — wax ages with time and sunlight, not with the odometer.

Rainy season deserves its own rule: lay down a coat before it starts and another once it ends. The first helps water sheet off instead of sitting on the paint, and the second replaces what the rain took away.

## Why the range is so wide

### Sun and heat

Rain is not the main enemy — the sun is. Prolonged exposure dries out a wax film and flattens the gloss, which is why better waxes include UV protection in the formula. The same jar will behave very differently on a car baking in a parking lot than on one kept in a basement. Any durability figure printed on a product is a reference under normal conditions, not a guarantee.

### Acid rain, bird droppings and tree sap

Water on its own does little harm. What hurts is what it leaves behind when it evaporates: acidic residue and settled dust. Bird droppings and sap are more urgent still — a day or two in strong sun is enough to leave a mark. Remove them as soon as you see them rather than waiting for your next wax day.

### How you wash

An aggressive detergent, or an automatic wash with stiff brushes, can strip most of a fresh coat in a single visit. If you want your wax to last, use a pH-neutral shampoo, work with the two-bucket method, and dry with microfiber instead of dragging an old towel across dry paint.

## Three checks that beat the calendar

Spend thirty seconds after each wash and the car will tell you when it is time:

### 1. The water test

Rinse a panel and watch. Tight, rounded beads that roll off at the slightest tilt mean the film is intact. Water that flattens into a sheet and crawls down the panel means you are due.

### 2. The touch test

With the car washed and dried, glide a clean hand across the hood. Slick and smooth means there is still something protecting the surface. Gritty or draggy usually means iron particles and oil film have bonded to the paint — at that point, decontaminate or clay the panel before you wax.

### 3. The gloss test

Compare the front and rear of the car under the same light. Dark colours show it first: protected areas still look wet, while spent areas look dry and hazy. The hood and roof almost always fade first.

## Is frequent waxing bad for the paint?

As long as the wax contains no abrasives, applying it more often will not wear the finish. Swirl marks almost always trace back to the steps before the wax:

- Waxing over grit that was never rinsed off, which is effectively sanding the panel.
- Using one towel for the whole car, carrying sand from the rocker panels up to the hood.
- Working in direct sun on hot metal, so the wax flashes off and you press harder to remove it.

Get the wash, the dry and the panel-by-panel application right, and the interval matters far less than the technique.

## Habits that stretch the interval

- Keep a microfiber towel and a quick detailer in the car so droppings and sap can be lifted early, never rubbed off dry.
- Wash on an overcast day or in the early morning, when the bodywork is cool.
- Always dry the car. Water spots baked onto paint do more harm than skipping the wash.
- Park indoors when you can. If outdoors is the only option, at least avoid spots under trees or beneath air-conditioner drains.

Do those consistently and a single jar of wax will feel like it lasts noticeably longer — and waxing shifts from damage control to simple maintenance.
